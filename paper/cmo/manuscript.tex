\begin{abstract}
Common Main Objective (CMO) stereo microscopes violate the single-optical-centre assumption of standard camera calibration, making joint estimation of optical parameters and board poses severely ill-conditioned (Schur-complement coupling norm $0.81$).
We present a two-stage approach: first, a Zernike rayfield---a per-pixel 3D line---is fitted to ChArUco corner observations, decoupling measurement from model identification (coupling norm $0.0$); second, a compact physical model is constructed by iterative residual analysis, where the Zernike decomposition of the residual between candidate model and measured rayfield reveals which physical degree of freedom is missing.
On 10 stereo pairs from the open Pycaso dataset, the final 26-parameter model---a quasi-telecentric CMO skeleton with per-channel SE(3) arm alignment---achieves \SI{1.06}{px} reprojection error (P50=\SI{0.87}{px}, P95=\SI{1.84}{px}), compared to $>300$~px for standard OpenCV stereo calibration.
A ray-space BIC comparison with operational reprojection guard selects this model as the best usable physical model ($\Delta\text{BIC}>40\,000$ over pinhole, Brown-Conrady, and parallel-plate alternatives).
The Zernike rayfield serves not merely as a calibration model but as a diagnostic instrument: it tells the user \emph{which} physical degree of freedom is missing, guiding hypothesis refinement through residual structure rather than blind fitting.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} stereo microscope; CMO; non-central calibration; Zernike rayfield; BIC model selection; telecentricity

\section{Introduction}
% ======================================================================

Stereo calibration is a mature technology when the camera obeys the pinhole model: all chief rays pass through a single optical centre, and mature toolchains (OpenCV, MATLAB, Bouguet) produce reliable results.
This assumption breaks down for Common Main Objective (CMO) stereo microscopes, which use a single large objective shared between two off-axis sub-apertures \cite{inoue1997}.
Each channel's chief rays appear to originate from a different effective sub-pupil, separated by the stereo baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:cmo}).
OpenCV's \texttt{stereoCalibrate} \cite{opencv} assumes one optical centre per camera; on the CMO architecture it produces unusable results ($>300$~px reprojection error under the tested configuration).

\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

The broader problem is that many optical instruments---CMO and Greenough stereo microscopes, endoscopes, Scheimpflug cameras, multi-camera arrays---depart from the central projection model.
Generic camera models \cite{grossberg2001,sturm2004,schops2020} and recent stereo microscope calibration studies \cite{liu2023,pan2024} highlight the need for non-central approaches, but do not provide a systematic method for identifying the correct physical model family from observations (see Section~2 for a detailed review).

StereoComplex \cite{stereocomplex} addresses this gap: it fits a Zernike rayfield $\mathcal{R}(u,v) = (O(u,v), d(u,v))$---a 3D line for every pixel---to corner observations, using a Zernike polynomial basis \cite{zernike1934,noll1976}, widely used in optical testing and metrology \cite{wang2022,li2024}.
This two-stage decomposition---measure rays first, identify optics second---is the central insight: it decouples the severely ill-conditioned joint estimation of optical parameters and board poses into a well-conditioned rayfield fit followed by pose-free model selection \cite{schops2020}.
From this \emph{measured} rayfield, physical descriptors can be read directly (Section~3), structural mismatches diagnosed by residual modal analysis (Section~3.3), and compact physical models constructed iteratively (Section~3.4--3.5).

This paper presents the first real-data validation of this workflow on a CMO stereo microscope using legacy ChArUco \cite{garrido2014,romero2018} images from the open Pycaso dataset \cite{pycaso2023}.
The contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A complete pipeline from raw ChArUco images to a compact 26-parameter physical CMO model, validated on real data.
  \item A residual analysis method---Zernike modal decomposition of direction and moment differences between a candidate physical model and the measured rayfield---that identifies missing degrees of freedom.
  \item A BIC-based model selection framework with an operational reprojection guard that prevents ray-space-optimal but pixel-unusable models from being selected.
  \item Demonstration that the Zernike rayfield is an excellent initialiser for direct corner bundle adjustment (only \SI{7}{\percent} improvement over the rayfield-initialised parameters).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Generic and Non-Central Camera Calibration}

The pinhole camera model with polynomial distortion \cite{zhang2000,brown1966} remains the standard in computer vision toolkits \cite{opencv,bouguet2008}.
For wide-angle and fisheye lenses, unified models \cite{mei2007,kannala2006,scaramuzza2006} extend the pinhole paradigm with additional radial terms.
However, these parametric models share a fundamental assumption: all chief rays pass through a single optical centre.

For cameras that violate this assumption, generic (non-parametric) models assign a per-pixel ray without imposing a central projection constraint.
Grossberg and Nayar \cite{grossberg2001} introduced the raxel model, treating each pixel as an independent ray.
Sturm and Ramalingam \cite{sturm2004,ramalingam2017} developed a generic calibration framework from multiple views of a planar target, showing that a flexible pixel-to-ray mapping can be estimated without knowing the camera model \emph{a priori}.
Miraldo and Araujo \cite{miraldo2018} proposed smooth camera models using B-spline interpolation.
Soloff et al.\ \cite{soloff1997} used a polynomial expansion for 3D particle image velocimetry.
Camposeco et al.\ \cite{camposeco2016} demonstrated spline-based generic calibration with subpixel accuracy.
Ramalingam, Sturm, and Nayar \cite{ramalingam2010} unified these approaches in a generic structure-from-motion framework.

Most recently, Sch\"ops et al.\ \cite{schops2020} showed that highly parameterised generic models (10,000+ parameters) can outperform classical 12-parameter models on standard benchmarks, challenging the assumption that parametric models are inherently more robust.
Larsson et al.\ \cite{larsson2024} extended this with B-spline field models that achieve state-of-the-art generic calibration with compact representations.
Deep learning approaches \cite{jin2023,zhuang2023} learn calibration from images but require large training datasets and lack physical interpretability.

Our work differs from these generic approaches in two ways: (1) we use the Zernike rayfield not as a final calibration model but as a \emph{diagnostic instrument} to discover the correct physical model family, and (2) we construct a compact, interpretable physical model guided by residual structure rather than imposing a generic non-parametric fit.

\subsection{Stereo Microscope Calibration}

Stereo microscopes pose a specific calibration challenge: the two optical channels share a common main objective but have separate effective pupils.
Werner et al.\ \cite{werner2014} calibrated a stereo microscope using a precision 3D target with known features, achieving micrometre accuracy but requiring specialised hardware.
Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2023} proposed a hybrid ray-based model combining a pinhole backbone with ray corrections, validated on a surgical microscope.
Wei et al.\ \cite{wei2024} used a portable phase target for flexible stereo microscope calibration.
Pan et al.\ \cite{pan2024} integrated structured light with a stereo microscope for self-calibrating 3D measurement.

These methods calibrate a specific instrument but do not address the generic problem of \emph{identifying} the correct optical model family from observations.
Our contribution is a systematic framework that measures the ray-field first, then uses residual analysis to construct a physical model that matches the observed structure---here, discovering the telecentric CMO family and the SE(3) arm misalignment.

\subsection{Model Selection in Camera Calibration}

Model selection in calibration traditionally relies on reprojection RMS, which penalises underfitting but is insensitive to overfitting.
The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) \cite{akaike1974} and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) \cite{schwarz1978} add parameter-count penalties, providing a principled trade-off between fit quality and model complexity.
Strecha et al.\ \cite{strecha2008} applied BIC to benchmark camera calibration and multi-view stereo.
Our work extends BIC-based model selection to ray space: we compare optical model families (pinhole, Brown-Conrady, parallel-plate, telecentric CMO) against a measured Zernike rayfield, and add an operational reprojection guard that rejects models whose pixel RMS exceeds a usability threshold---a constraint that the raw ray-space BIC does not capture.

\subsection{Pl\"ucker Coordinates and Line-Based Geometry}

Pl\"ucker coordinates \cite{plucker1865} represent a 3D line as a 6-vector $(d, m)$ where $d$ is the direction and $m = O \times d$ is the moment.
They are widely used in geometric computer vision for line-based structure from motion and calibration \cite{triggs1999}.
Our residual analysis uses Pl\"ucker moments to separate direction errors from origin errors: $\Delta d$ reveals angular misalignment; $\Delta m$ reveals the effective ray origin shift.
The $Z_0^0$-dominated residual in both $\Delta d$ and $\Delta m$ is the key diagnostic that guides the SE(3) arm alignment.

\subsection{Residual-Guided Model Construction}

The idea of using residuals to guide model refinement has roots in adaptive optics and wavefront sensing \cite{zernike1934,noll1976}, where Zernike decomposition of a distorted wavefront identifies specific aberrations (defocus, astigmatism, coma) that are then corrected by deformable mirrors.
Our method applies the same principle to camera calibration: the Zernike decomposition of the residual between a candidate physical model and the measured rayfield identifies which physical degree of freedom is missing---a $Z_0^0$-dominated residual points to a global offset (SE(3) arm misalignment); a $Z_2$-dominated residual would point to astigmatism or field curvature.
This is distinct from generic non-parametric approaches \cite{schops2020,larsson2024} that fit a flexible model without extracting physical meaning from the residual structure.

\section{Method}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{Zernike Order Selection by BIC}
\label{sec:bic_order}

The Zernike rayfield has two hyperparameters: the origin polynomial order $o$ and the direction polynomial order $d$.
Increasing either adds Zernike modes, reducing pixel RMS at the cost of more parameters.
We sweep $o \in \{0,1,2\}$ and $d \in \{2,3,4\}$ (7 combinations) and select the optimal order by BIC \cite{schwarz1978}:

\begin{equation}
\text{BIC}_{\text{px}} = k \log(N_{\text{obs}}) + N_{\text{obs}} \log(\text{RSS}_{\text{px}} / N_{\text{obs}}),
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the total number of parameters (Zernike coefficients + poses), $N_{\text{obs}} = 3300$ corner observations, and $\text{RSS}_{\text{px}}$ is the sum of squared pixel-equivalent residuals.

The BIC minimum at O(1)+d(3) achieves \SI{0.41}{px} RMS with 93~parameters.
We nevertheless select O(0)+d(2) (57~params, \SI{0.47}{px}) as the preferred rayfield configuration, for three reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Simplicity:} 57~params vs.\ 93 --- nearly half the parameters for a penalty of only \SI{0.06}{px} in RMS ($\sim$0.1~px in practice).
  \item \textbf{Marginal improvement:} the residual gain from d(2) to d(3) (\SI{0.47}{px} $\to$ \SI{0.41}{px}) is well within typical subpixel noise of ChArUco detection; the additional BIC improvement does not justify the extra model complexity.
  \item \textbf{Consistency with the physical model:} the CMO physical model assumes rigid sub-pupils ($o=0$). Using the same origin order in the Zernike rayfield makes the two representations internally consistent and comparable: the Zernike field measures what the physical model can represent.
\end{enumerate}

Selecting O(0)+d(2) achieves \SI{90}{\percent} of the noise-floor accuracy with a representation that is internally consistent with the physical model it validates.

\subsection{Per-Pixel Ray-Field and Transverse Gauge}

For each pixel $(u,v)$ on the sensor, the imaging system defines a 3D line in space.
We represent this line $\mathcal{L}_c(u,v)$ by an origin point $O_c(u,v) \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a unit direction vector $d_c(u,v) \in \mathbb{S}^2$, where $c \in \{L,R\}$ denotes the left or right channel.
Points on the line are parameterised as $O_c + t\,d_c$ for $t \in \mathbb{R}$.

The origin $O_c(u,v)$ is not unique: any point along the ray would represent the same line.
We fix this gauge freedom by enforcing the \textbf{transverse gauge} $O_c \cdot d_c = 0$, which selects the point on the ray closest to the coordinate origin.
This eliminates one redundant degree of freedom per ray and stabilises the parameterisation.

The origin and direction fields are expanded on a Zernike polynomial basis \cite{zernike1934,noll1976}:
\begin{equation}
O_c(u,v) = \sum_{m} a_{c,m}^O \, Z_m(\tilde{u},\tilde{v}), \qquad
d_c(u,v) = \operatorname{normalize}\!\left(d_c^{\text{pinhole}}(u,v) + \sum_{m \le d_{\text{max}}} a_{c,m}^d \, Z_m(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})\right),
\end{equation}
where $Z_m$ are the real-valued Zernike polynomials on the unit disk, $\tilde{u},\tilde{v}$ are normalised pixel coordinates mapping the sensor to $[-1,1]^2$, $d_c^{\text{pinhole}}$ is the initial pinhole direction from a fixed central projection matrix $K$, and the direction perturbation is projected transverse to $d_c^{\text{pinhole}}$ before renormalisation.
The expansion order for the origin field is $o_{\text{max}}$ and for the direction field is $d_{\text{max}}$; selecting $o_{\text{max}}=0$ and $d_{\text{max}}=2$ (see Section~2.1) gives 3 origin coefficients and 18 direction coefficients per channel.

\subsection{Ray-Space Bundle Adjustment}

The rayfield coefficients $a_{c,m}^O, a_{c,m}^d$ and the board poses $\{\eta_p\}_{p=1}^{P}$ are estimated jointly from $N$ ChArUco corner observations.
Each observation $k$ links a pixel $(u_k, v_k)$ on channel $c_k$ to a known 3D board point $X_k$ expressed in the board frame.
The board pose $\eta_p = (R_p, t_p) \in \text{SE}(3)$ transforms $X_k$ to the camera frame: $X_k^{\text{cam}} = R_p X_k + t_p$.

The \textbf{ray-space residual} measures the perpendicular distance from the transformed board point to the ray $\mathcal{L}_{c_k}(u_k,v_k)$:
\begin{equation}
r_k = \| (X_k^{\text{cam}} - O_{c_k}) - ((X_k^{\text{cam}} - O_{c_k})\cdot d_{c_k})\, d_{c_k} \|.
\end{equation}
This is the norm of the component of $X_k^{\text{cam}} - O_{c_k}$ orthogonal to the ray direction $d_{c_k}$.
The objective is the sum of squared residuals:
\begin{equation}
\min_{\{a_{c,m}\}, \{\eta_p\}} \sum_k r_k^2,
\end{equation}
solved by nonlinear least-squares (Levenberg-Marquardt).

For the CMO microscope, we impose the physically-motivated constraint that the board is mounted on a Z-only translation stage: all $P$ frames share a common rotation $R$ and XY translation $(t_x, t_y)$, with only the Z component varying per frame.
This reduces the pose parameters from $6P$ to $3 + 2 + P = 15$ (for $P=10$ frames: 60 $\to$ 15), making the 57-parameter Zernike BA well-conditioned.

The ray-space BA differs from classical reprojection minimisation \cite{zhang2000} in a fundamental way: it optimises the 3D geometric consistency of rays rather than the 2D reprojection error.
This is the measurement stage of the two-stage decomposition (see Section~4.2).

\subsection{The Zernike Rayfield as Observable}

The fitted rayfield $\mathcal{R}_c(u,v) = (O_c(u,v), d_c(u,v))$ is the experimental observable.
From the centre-pixel ray, four rayfield-internal geometric descriptors are read directly---no physical model fit required:
$b = \|O_R - O_L\| = \SI{24.9}{mm}$ (stereo baseline),
$z_p = (|O_{L,z}| + |O_{R,z}|)/2 = \SI{2.5}{mm}$ (sub-pupil depth),
$WD = \SI{64.7}{mm}$ (working distance, from mean pose Z),
$f_{\text{obj}} = WD - z_p = \SI{62.2}{mm}$ (objective focal length),
$\theta = \arccos(d_L \cdot d_R) = \SI{22.6}{\degree}$ (convergence angle).

\subsection{Physical Model Construction by Residual Analysis}

The core methodology is iterative: propose a candidate physical model, compute its residual against the Zernike rayfield, decompose the residual on Zernike modes, and use the dominant mode to identify the missing degree of freedom.

\subsubsection{Perspective CMO (Baseline)}

The simplest CMO model places a perspective camera behind each decentred sub-pupil $S_c = (\pm b/2, 0, WD - f_{\text{obj}})$.
This predicts $d_y(u,v) \propto (v - c_y)$---a strong linear gradient.
The measured $d_y$ field is nearly constant across the sensor (range $0.079$, mean $+0.059$), while the perspective model predicts a range of $0.232$---a $3\times$ discrepancy (Figure~\ref{fig:dy}).
The real system is \textbf{object-space telecentric}, not perspective.

The Zernike rayfield (measured) shows a nearly constant $d_y$ (range $0.079$), while the perspective CMO model predicts a $3\times$ larger linear gradient (range $0.232$). The telecentric model reproduces the measured profile almost exactly.}

\subsubsection{Telecentric CMO}

The perspective model fails because the real microscope is object-space telecentric: chief rays are nearly parallel rather than diverging from a point.
The telecentric model directly parameterises the ray-field without deriving it from a point projection.
For each channel $c \in \{L,R\}$, the origin is a rigid sub-pupil:
\begin{equation}
O_c = S_c = (\pm b/2,\; 0,\; WD - f_{\text{obj}}),
\end{equation}
where the sign is $+$ for right and $-$ for left.
The direction field is an affine function of normalised angular coordinates $\tilde{u} = (u - c_x)p/f_{\text{ang}}$, $\tilde{v} = (v - c_y)p/f_{\text{ang}}$, where $p$ is the pixel pitch, $f_{\text{ang}}$ is the angular focal length, and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the principal point:
\begin{equation}
d_c(u,v) = \operatorname{normalize}\!\left(d_{c,0} + s_{x,c}\,\tilde{u}\,e_x + s_{y,c}\,\tilde{v}\,e_y + s_{xy,c}\,\tilde{u}\,\tilde{v}\,e_x + s_{yx,c}\,\tilde{v}\,\tilde{u}\,e_y\right),
\end{equation}
where $d_{c,0} = (\pm\sin\theta,\; d_y^{\text{com}},\; \cos\theta)$ is the chief-ray direction, with $\theta$ the convergence half-angle and $d_y^{\text{com}}$ a shared vertical component.

Adding pupil shear---an affine transverse variation of the origin---completes the model:
\begin{equation}
O_c(u,v) = S_c + P_{d_c}^\perp(\rho_{x,c}\,\tilde{u}\,e_x + \rho_{y,c}\,\tilde{v}\,e_y),
\end{equation}
where $P_{d}^\perp v = v - (v\cdot d)\,d$ projects onto the plane orthogonal to $d$, ensuring $O_c \cdot d_c = 0$.

The 14 parameters are: $f_{\text{obj}}$, $WD$, $b$ (skeleton), $c_x$, $c_y$ (principal point), $f_{\text{ang}}$ (angular scale), $\theta$, $d_y^{\text{com}}$ (chief-ray direction), $s_{x,L}$, $s_{y,L}$, $s_{x,R}$, $s_{y,R}$ (per-channel slopes), $\rho_x$, $\rho_y$ (shared pupil shear).
This model achieves \SI{0.12}{mm} ray-space RMS (down from \SI{3.48}{mm} for perspective) and \SI{14.6}{px} reprojection (down from \SI{86}{px}).
The dominant geometry is captured, but \SI{14.6}{px} is far from the Zernike reference (\SI{0.47}{px}).

\subsubsection{Residual Analysis}

The residual between the telecentric model and the Zernike rayfield is computed on a $41\times41$ grid:
$\Delta d = d_{\text{Zernike}} - d_{\text{CMO}}$,
$\Delta m = m_{\text{Zernike}} - m_{\text{CMO}}$,
where $m = O \times d$ is the Pl\"ucker moment \cite{plucker1865}.

Projecting on Zernike modes up to order~4 reveals that \textbf{both $\Delta d$ and $\Delta m$ are 97--98\,\% $Z_0^0$ (global piston)}.
This is the crucial diagnostic: the residual is not a spatial field distortion (which would appear in higher Zernike modes) but a \textbf{global line-bundle offset}---the entire set of rays from each channel is slightly rotated or translated relative to the Zernike reference.

\subsubsection{Testing Alternative Hypotheses}

Two alternative hypotheses are tested and rejected:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Image-space pre-warp:} a polynomial mapping $\xi = W(u,v)$ before the direction model. Affine and quadratic warps \emph{degrade} pixel RMS (14.6 $\to$ 16.0--16.5~px).
  \item \textbf{Spatially varying origin:} affine and quadratic transverse origin fields with direction fixed. Only \SI{8}{\percent} ray RMS improvement---the residual is not spatial.
\end{itemize}

Both negative results are consistent with the $Z_0^0$ diagnostic (they would produce spatial, not global, changes).

\subsubsection{Residual-Guided Hypothesis Construction}

The central methodological contribution is that physical model construction is guided by \emph{residual structure}, not blind fitting.
At each stage, the direction residual $\Delta d = d_{\text{Zernike}} - d_{\text{model}}$ is computed on a $31\times31$ grid and displayed as a heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:residuals}).
The pattern of the residual directly reveals which physical degree of freedom is missing.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/residual_evolution.png}
\caption{Residual evolution across model stages. Direction error $\Delta d$ (degrees) between the Zernike rayfield (reference) and each candidate model, evaluated on a $31\times31$ pixel grid.
\textbf{Left:} Perspective CMO --- large structured error (RMS \SI{23.6}{\degree}, max \SI{27.7}{\degree}), dominated by a strong $d_y$ gradient.
\textbf{Centre:} Telecentric CMO --- RMS drops to \SI{0.27}{\degree} (two orders of magnitude), but the residual is still $Z_0^0$-dominated (global piston), revealing the missing SE(3) arm alignment.
\textbf{Right:} CMO + SE(3) --- RMS drops to \SI{0.003}{\degree}, near the noise floor.
The residual heatmap at each stage directly tells the user \emph{what} is wrong and \emph{where} to look next.}
\label{fig:residuals}
\end{figure}

Stage~1 (Perspective CMO): the residual is large and structured, with RMS \SI{23.6}{\degree} (max \SI{27.7}{\degree}).
The $d_y$ component shows a strong linear gradient trace---perspective projection from a point sub-pupil---that the Zernike field does not have.
This \textbf{rejects} the perspective family and points to object-space telecentricity.

Stage~2 (Telecentric CMO): the residual drops to \SI{0.27}{\degree} RMS (two orders of magnitude), confirming that the telecentric model family is correct.
However, the residual is $Z_0^0$-dominated (97--98\,\% global piston in both $\Delta d$ and $\Delta m$)---a constant offset across the entire field.
This \textbf{reveals} that the missing degree of freedom is a global line-bundle misalignment, not a spatial field distortion.

Stage~3 (CMO + SE(3)): the residual drops further to \SI{0.003}{\degree} RMS, near the noise floor.
The remaining gap to the Zernike reference (\SI{0.47}{px} vs \SI{1.06}{px}) comes from distributed low-amplitude aberrations (field curvature, astigmatism) that require the flexibility of the 57-parameter Zernike model.

The critical point is that \textbf{at no stage was a model added blindly}.
The heatmap pattern at each stage dictates the next hypothesis: perspective $\to$ telecentric (because the $d_y$ gradient is wrong), telecentric $\to$ SE(3) (because the residual is $Z_0^0$ piston, not spatial).

\subsubsection{Per-Channel SE(3) Arm Alignment}

The $Z_0^0$-dominated residual points to a global misalignment of each optical arm.
A rigid transform $(R_c, t_c) \in \text{SE}(3)$ is applied to each channel's ray bundle, representing a small rotation and translation of the entire optical arm relative to the ideal CMO skeleton.
For each pixel $(u,v)$ on channel $c$, the telecentric ray $(O_c^{\text{tel}}, d_c^{\text{tel}})$ is transformed:
\begin{equation}
d_c(u,v) = R_c \, d_c^{\text{tel}}(u,v), \qquad
O_c(u,v) = R_c \, O_c^{\text{tel}}(u,v) + t_c.
\end{equation}
Each $R_c \in \text{SO}(3)$ is parameterised by a 3-element rotation vector $r_c \in \mathbb{R}^3$ ($\theta_c = \|r_c\|$, axis $r_c/\|r_c\|$).
The 12 additional parameters ($r_L, t_L, r_R, t_R$) bring the total to 26.

Jointly fitting the 14 telecentric parameters and the 12 SE(3) parameters against the Zernike rayfield reduces pixel RMS from \SI{14.6}{px} to \textbf{\SI{1.06}{px}} (P50=\SI{0.87}{px}, P95=\SI{1.84}{px})---a $14\times$ improvement.
The fitted rotations are $\sim$\SI{2.5}{\degree} (left) and $\sim$\SI{3.7}{\degree} (right); translations are sub-mm.
The rotation angles are stable across optimisation runs; the translation components trade off with the telecentric base parameters (a known identifiability limitation).

\subsection{Model Selection}

Ray-space BIC \cite{schwarz1978} identifies the correct optical family among five candidates (pinhole, Brown-Conrady, parallel-plate, telecentric CMO with/without shear).
The telecentric CMO+shear model wins by $>40\,000$ BIC points over all alternatives.

However, this 14-parameter model produces \SI{14.6}{px} reprojection---it is the correct \emph{family} but not a \emph{usable} model.
We introduce an \textbf{operational BIC} that adds a hard reprojection guard:
\begin{equation}
\text{BIC}_{\text{usable}} = \text{BIC}_{\text{ray}} + \mathbf{1}_{e_{\text{px}} > \SI{1.5}{px}} \left[10^6 + N_{\text{px}} \log\left(\frac{e_{\text{px}}^2}{1.5^2}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
The 14p telecentric model is rejected ($\text{BIC}_{\text{usable}} \approx +978\,890$); the 26p SE(3)-aligned model is selected as best usable ($\text{BIC}_{\text{usable}} \approx -32\,433$).

\subsection{Why Direct Bundle Adjustment is Ill-Conditioned for Non-Central Optics}

The two-stage decomposition strategy (rayfield measurement $\to$ model identification) is motivated by a fundamental conditioning problem in direct bundle adjustment (BA).
We formalise this using the Schur complement of the Fisher information matrix.

\subsubsection{Conditioning of the Joint Estimation Problem}

Direct BA jointly estimates optical parameters $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and board poses $\eta \in \mathbb{R}^q$ ($q = 6P$ for $P$ frames) by minimising the pixel reprojection error.
The Fisher information matrix at the optimum has the block structure:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}(\theta,\eta) =
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathcal{I}_{\theta\theta} & \mathcal{I}_{\theta\eta} \\[2pt]
\mathcal{I}_{\eta\theta} & \mathcal{I}_{\eta\eta}
\end{bmatrix}
=
J^T J,
\end{equation}
where $J = \partial r / \partial(\theta,\eta)$ is the residual Jacobian.
The $\mathcal{I}_{\theta\theta}$ block captures the information about optics alone; $\mathcal{I}_{\eta\eta}$ captures poses; $\mathcal{I}_{\theta\eta}$ captures their cross-coupling.

When $\mathcal{I}_{\theta\eta} \neq 0$, the optics and pose estimates are coupled: a small error in pose estimation propagates to the optics estimate and vice versa.
The effective information about $\theta$ after marginalising over the uncertain poses is the \textbf{Schur complement}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_{\theta|\eta} = \mathcal{I}_{\theta\theta} - \mathcal{I}_{\theta\eta}\,\mathcal{I}_{\eta\eta}^{-1}\,\mathcal{I}_{\eta\theta}.
\end{equation}

We define the \textbf{coupling norm} $c \in [0,1]$ as the fraction of the optics information that is lost to pose coupling:
\begin{equation}
c = \frac{\|\mathcal{I}_{\theta\eta}\,\mathcal{I}_{\eta\eta}^{-1}\,\mathcal{I}_{\eta\theta}\|_F}{\|\mathcal{I}_{\theta\theta}\|_F},
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_F$ is the Frobenius norm.
When $c \approx 0$, optics and poses are decoupled (well-conditioned estimation).
When $c \approx 1$, optics and poses are nearly inseparable (ill-conditioned).

\subsubsection{Empirical Coupling Values}

For the Pycaso CMO direct bundle adjustment (19 optical $+$ 12 pose parameters), the Schur-complement coupling norm is $c = 0.81$, computed from the finite-difference Jacobian at the converged optimum.
This means that 81\,\% of the apparent information about optical parameters is absorbed by pose coupling---the two parameter blocks are nearly inseparable.
By contrast, the rayfield-mediated pipeline operates with $c = 0.0$: the Zernike BA absorbs pose uncertainty into the rayfield through its constrained shared-rotation model (15 pose parameters), and the ray-space model identification stage is entirely pose-free.

The coupling norm $c = 0.81$ quantifies why direct BA fails to converge on the Pycaso CMO oracle (RMS \SI{29}{px}, Section~\ref{sec:results}): the optimiser cannot separate optical parameters from pose parameters, and the pinhole-based initialisation ($\approx$\SI{2}{px} error) is too far from the true solution.
Schur-coupling values for the other oracles (pinhole, Brown-Conrady, parallel-plate, Greenough) were not reliably computable from the current diagnostic infrastructure and are therefore not reported.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/schur_singular_values.png}
\caption{Singular value spectra of the optics information matrix $\mathcal{I}_{\theta\theta}$ (direct BA), its Schur complement $\mathcal{I}_{\theta|\eta}$ after marginalising poses, and the rayfield BA (pose-free). The trailing singular values of $\mathcal{I}_{\theta\theta}$ collapse in the Schur complement, indicating that the last $\sim$8 optical parameters are nearly unobservable when poses are jointly estimated. \end{figure}

\subsection{Corner Bundle Adjustment}

The 26p model, fitted to the rayfield, is refined by direct corner bundle adjustment (joint optimisation of model parameters and per-frame poses on 3300 corner observations).
The improvement is only \SI{7}{\percent} (\SI{1.06}{px} $\to$ \SI{0.98}{px}), confirming that the rayfield-initialised parameters are already near-optimal for corner reprojection.
This is a strong validation of the two-stage decomposition strategy: the rayfield fit, which never directly minimises corner error, produces parameters so close to the corner optimum that the dedicated BA can barely improve them.
In contrast, the direct approach---jointly estimating optics and poses from corners---is severely ill-conditioned for this non-central system (Schur-complement coupling norm $0.81$), failing to converge with a pinhole initialisation (RMS \SI{29}{px}).

\subsection{Dataset and Pipeline}

Ten stereo pairs of $2048\times2048$~px ChArUco \cite{garrido2014,romero2018} images (16$\times$12 squares, \SI{0.3}{mm}, DICT\_6X6\_250, \texttt{setLegacyPattern(True)}) from the Pycaso open dataset \cite{pycaso2023} are used.
The Z-range is \SI{2.65}{mm}--\SI{3.35}{mm} ($\Delta = \SI{0.70}{mm}$).
Corner detection yields 61--161 corners per frame; Hessian-based completion ($|\det H|$ + Otsu + barycentre) fills all 165.
A double Ray2D TPS denoising pass \cite{bookstein1989} (ArUco markers at $\lambda=10$, then completed 165 corners at $\lambda=3$, Huber $c=1.5$) produces gauge-stable 2D corners.
The two-phase TPS is not cosmetic: without it, raw ChArUco detections carry structured biases from compression artifacts, blur, and defocus that cause the Zernike rayfield fit to become gauge-unstable---the direction piston $Z_0$ drifts by \SI{8.5}{\degree} between constrained and full-pose fits, making physical interpretation impossible.
After double TPS, the gauge drift drops to \SI{0.023}{\degree}, and the rayfield becomes a stable experimental oracle \cite{stereocomplex}.

\subsection{OpenCV Tuning Does Not Recover}

We tested the full OpenCV stereo calibration flag space: \texttt{CALIB\_RATIONAL\_MODEL} (8 distortion coefficients), \texttt{CALIB\_THIN\_PRISM}, and \texttt{CALIB\_TILTED\_MODEL}.
None of these configurations produced a usable calibration on the CMO data.
The rational model, with the most parameters (14 intrinsics per camera), still fails because the distortion model remains radial and centred---it cannot represent a non-central projection where rays originate from two different sub-pupils.
The best OpenCV configuration achieves $>$\SI{300}{px} RMS, compared to \SI{1.06}{px} for our 26-parameter CMO+SE(3) model.
This confirms that the failure is structural (central projection assumption), not a matter of under-parameterised distortion.

\subsection{Geometric Descriptor Validation}

The Pycaso software publication \cite{pycaso2023} documents a Leica~205C stereo microscope. However, nominal geometric parameters (baseline, working distance, focal length) of the specific instrument used to acquire the open dataset are not available from the publication or manufacturer datasheets.
Our rayfield-derived descriptors---$b = \SI{24.9}{mm}$, $WD = \SI{64.7}{mm}$, $f_{\text{obj}} = \SI{62.2}{mm}$---are substantially different.
This indicates that the open Pycaso calibration dataset was produced with a different microscope or optical configuration than the one documented in the Pycaso paper.

While we cannot validate against the nominal specifications of the actual microscope used (unknown manufacturer, unknown model), the StereoComplex-derived descriptors are \textbf{internally consistent} by three independent tests:
(1)~cross-validation stability: $1.07 \pm 0.02$~px RMS across 5 folds, with descriptor spread $\pm 0.4$~mm (baseline), $\pm 0.15$~mm (WD);
(2)~bootstrap 95\,\% confidence intervals: $b \in [23.48, 25.04]$~mm, $WD \in [64.64, 65.13]$~mm, $\theta \in [21.04^\circ, 22.72^\circ]$;
(3)~frame-count stability: descriptors vary by $< 0.2$~mm (baseline) and $< 0.1$~mm (WD) from 3 to 10 frames (Table~\ref{tab:pose_sweep}).

External validation remains open.
Confirming the absolute accuracy of the geometric descriptors requires either (a)~manufacturer specifications for the specific microscope and optical configuration used to collect the Pycaso calibration images, or (b)~an independent 3D measurement of the sub-pupil positions and working distance.
Both are beyond the scope of this study and remain a limitation (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Comparison with Generic Methods}

We did not benchmark against Sch\"ops et al.\ \cite{schops2020} or Larsson et al.\ \cite{larsson2024} on this dataset because their code targets pinhole-based initialisation and central projection with high-parameter-count spline corrections.
Applying these methods to a non-central CMO microscope would require adapting their initialisation strategy to handle two sub-pupils, which is beyond the scope of this comparison.
We expect that a generic spline model with sufficient parameters could match the Zernike rayfield's \SI{0.47}{px} RMS, but at the cost of thousands of non-interpretable parameters, compared to our 26 interpretable parameters.

\subsection{Internal Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Three internal validation experiments assess the robustness of the 26p pipeline on the Pycaso dataset.

\textbf{Cross-validation.}
5-fold leave-2-out cross-validation (fit on 8 frames, evaluate on 2 held-out frames) yields a mean pixel RMS of $1.07 \pm 0.02$~px.
The low standard deviation ($\sim$\SI{0.02}{px}) confirms that the model is not overfitting to specific frames; the calibration is stable across the 10-frame dataset.
Fold~1 shows slightly higher RMS (\SI{1.09}{px}) and a lower baseline estimate ($b = \SI{23.1}{mm}$) because the held-out frames (0 and 1) cover the most extreme Z values.

\textbf{Bootstrap descriptor stability.}
100 bootstrap iterations (resampling 10 frames with replacement, refitting the full pipeline each time) give 95\,\% confidence intervals:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Baseline $b$: [$24.2$, $25.4$]~mm (mean $24.6$~mm, $\sigma = 0.4$~mm)
  \item Working distance $WD$: [$64.7$, $65.1$]~mm (mean $64.9$~mm, $\sigma = 0.14$~mm)
    \item Convergence angle $\theta$: [$21.4^\circ$, $23.0^\circ$] (mean $22.2^\circ$, $\sigma = 0.4^\circ$)
\end{itemize}
The working distance is the most stable descriptor ($\sigma < \SI{0.2}{mm}$), while the baseline and convergence angle show \SI{0.4}{mm} and \SI{0.4}{\degree} variation respectively---consistent with the gauge sensitivity of the Zernike origin field noted in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\textbf{Sensitivity to fixed focal length $f_x$.}
Varying the fixed pinhole focal length $f_x$ by $\pm 10\%$ (5 values: 23040, 24320, 25600, 26880, 28160~px) produces:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pixel RMS: $1.21 \to 0.95$~px---remains near the one-pixel range, confirming that the rayfield is stable as a calibration object.
  \item Working distance: $58.3 \to 71.3$~mm---linearly proportional to the initial fx ($\pm 10\%$ fx $\to$ $\pm 10\%$ WD). WD is directly scaled by the pinhole reference and is \emph{not} independently identifiable.
  \item Convergence angle: $24.1^\circ \to 20.5^\circ$---varies inversely with WD.
  \item Baseline: $23.9 \to 25.1$~mm ($\pm 2.5\%$)---moderately stable.
\end{itemize}
The absolute physical descriptors, especially $WD$ and $\theta$, remain sensitive to the chosen pinhole reference scale.
Descriptor readout therefore requires either an independently known optical scale (pixel pitch, magnification) or external metrological validation---a known limitation of all single-view calibration methods without a known reference object.

\section{Discussion}
% ======================================================================

\subsection{The Rayfield as Diagnostic Instrument}

The key methodological contribution is the feedback loop: Ray2D preprocessing $\to$ Ray3D measurement $\to$ residual modal analysis $\to$ hypothesis $\to$ model refinement $\to$ verification.
Without the rayfield, the 2D reprojection error tells you \emph{that} the model is wrong but not \emph{how}.
The Zernike projection of $\Delta d$ and $\Delta m$ directly identifies whether the missing DOF is global (low-order Zernike modes) or spatial (high-order modes).

Figure~\ref{fig:residuals} illustrates this diagnostic value concretely.
The residual heatmap at each model stage is a \textbf{visual hypothesis generator}: the perspective CMO's structured $d_y$ error (\SI{23.6}{\degree} RMS) sends the user to telecentric models; the telecentric model's uniform $Z_0^0$ piston (\SI{0.27}{\degree} RMS) sends the user to global arm alignment; and the SE(3)-refined model's near-zero residual (\SI{0.003}{\degree} RMS) confirms convergence.
At no point does the user need to guess---the residual pattern at each stage dictates the next hypothesis.

In this case study, the $Z_0^0$-dominated residual correctly guided us to the SE(3) arm alignment, while alternative hypotheses (pre-warp, variable origin) were correctly rejected by the same diagnostic.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Single dataset.} Results are demonstrated on 10 stereo pairs from one Pycaso CMO microscope. Generalisation to other CMO designs, manufacturers, or magnification settings has not been demonstrated and requires additional validation.
  \item \textbf{No independent 3D ground truth.} All residuals (ray-space and pixel-equivalent) are computed on the same ChArUco board points used for calibration. There is no validation against a separate certified 3D object or an independently measured reference.
  \item \textbf{Physical descriptors are gauge-sensitive.} The geometric descriptors ($b$, $WD$, $f_{\text{obj}}$, $\theta$) are read from the rayfield under the transverse gauge $O \cdot d = 0$ and a fixed pinhole reference $f_x = 25600$. The working distance is linearly proportional to the initial $f_x$ ($\pm 10\%$ fx $\to$ $\pm 10\%$ WD, Section~\ref{sec:validation}), and is \emph{not} independently identifiable without an external scale reference.
  \item \textbf{SE(3) translation parameters are not uniquely identifiable.} The rotation angles ($\sim$\SI{2.5}{\degree}, $\sim$\SI{3.7}{\degree}) are stable across optimisation runs, but the translation components trade off with the telecentric base parameters (WD, $f_{\text{obj}}$, $b$, principal point). Only the SE(3) rotation is a robust diagnostic; the translation is effective rather than absolute.
  \item \textbf{The Zernike rayfield is a training residual.} Its \SI{0.0007}{mm} ray-space RMS is a self-evaluation on the support points it was fitted to; the \SI{0.47}{px} pixel RMS is a noise-floor estimate, not an independent measurement. The pixel RMS column in all tables provides the apples-to-apples comparison.
  \item \textbf{Fixed pinhole reference.} The Zernike BA uses a fixed $f_x = 25600$ and principal point at image centre. Errors in this reference propagate to the rayfield estimates and the derived physical descriptors.
  \item \textbf{Absolute metrological validation would require:} (a) manufacturer specifications for the actual microscope used, (b) an independent measurement of WD or baseline, or (c) a certified 3D calibration object at a known distance. None of these are available for the open Pycaso dataset.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Generalisability}

The methodology---measure with a flexible non-parametric basis, read physical descriptors, diagnose residual structure, build compact physical model, validate by BIC---is transferable to any non-standard optical instrument (endoscopes, Scheimpflug cameras, multi-camera arrays).
The SE(3) arm alignment is specific to CMO microscopes, but the residual analysis that identified it is general.

\subsection{Why the Two-Stage Decomposition Works}

The central insight of this work is not a specific model architecture but a problem decomposition strategy.
Direct bundle adjustment jointly estimates optical parameters $\theta$ and board poses $\eta$ from 2D corners; for non-central optics, the Schur complement of the reduced camera system \cite{stereocomplex} reveals a coupling norm of $0.81$, meaning optics and poses are nearly inseparable---the problem is severely ill-conditioned.
By inserting the Zernike rayfield as an intermediate variable, the estimation splits into two well-conditioned sub-problems: (1) fit a flexible rayfield to corners (Zernike BA, coupling norm $0.0$---poses are eliminated by the constrained-pose model), and (2) identify physical optics in ray space (pose-free model selection with BIC).

This decomposition is the mechanism behind all three empirical findings: the TPS pre-processing stabilises step (1) by cleaning the 2D input; the rayfield decouples step (2) from pose estimation; and the rayfield-initialised parameters lie so close to the corner optimum that direct BA can barely improve them.

\section{Conclusion}
% ======================================================================

We have demonstrated the first real-data validation of the StereoComplex non-central calibration pipeline on a CMO stereo microscope.
The key results are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A compact 26-parameter physical model (quasi-telecentric CMO skeleton + per-channel SE(3) arm alignment) achieves \SI{1.06}{px} reprojection (P50=\SI{0.87}{px}, P95=\SI{1.84}{px}) where standard OpenCV stereo calibration fails ($>300$~px).
  \item The Zernike rayfield serves as a diagnostic instrument: residual modal analysis identifies missing degrees of freedom, and the operational BIC with reprojection guard correctly selects the usable model.
  \item Physical descriptors---baseline, working distance, focal length, convergence angle---are read directly from the measured rays, without fitting a physical model.
  \item The rayfield-initialised parameters are near-optimal for direct corner bundle adjustment (only \SI{7}{\percent} improvement).
\end{itemize}

The CMO Pycaso case study validates a generalisable methodology: measure first, model second, diagnose by residual, refine by hypothesis testing.
Code, data, and documentation are publicly available at \url{https://github.com/jeffwitz/StereoComplex} (Zenodo DOI: \texttt{10.5281/zenodo.XXXXXXX}, to be updated upon acceptance).

\subsection*{Prospective}

Several directions extend this work.
First, the methodology should be validated on additional CMO microscope designs and manufacturers to confirm generalisability beyond the single Pycaso instrument.
Second, the Greenough stereo microscope family---with tilted optical axes rather than parallel sub-pupils---presents a harder identification problem where the telecentric model does not apply; the residual-guided construction method would need to discover a different geometric skeleton.
Third, quantitative uncertainty propagation from ChArUco corner noise to physical descriptor estimates (baseline, working distance) would strengthen the metrological claim.
Fourth, deep learning-based corner detection could replace the hand-tuned Hessian+TPS pipeline, potentially improving the raw 2D input quality and further reducing the gauge instability.
Finally, extending the Zernike rayfield to time-varying deformations (e.g., vibration, thermal drift) would enable \emph{in-situ} calibration monitoring without dedicated targets.
