\begin{abstract}
Stereo digital image correlation ultimately measures spatial differences of reconstructed motion, whereas stereo calibrations are commonly selected from image residuals or pointwise three-dimensional errors. This mismatch is especially relevant for common-main-objective (CMO) stereo microscopes, whose optical geometry is not naturally represented by two independent central cameras. We combine a compact ray-space identification of the CMO geometry with an explicit strain-space validation on archived rigid translations. A flexible Zernike ray field is first used as a geometric measuring instrument; its residual structure identifies a quasi-telecentric CMO model with per-channel rigid arm alignment. The resulting 26-parameter physical model reaches \SI{1.06}{px} calibration RMS on the real PYCASO configuration, compared with more than \SI{300}{px} for the tested conventional central stereo calibration. Mechanical validation then uses two independent 101-plane rigid translations, with 91 planes from each acquisition reserved for evaluation. On the fine \SI{0.08}{mm} sweep, a direct stereo-to-object polynomial gives the smallest nominal-depth discrepancy (\SI{5.20}{\micro\metre}) but develops an apparent-strain floor of approximately \SIrange{288}{301}{\micro\strain}, whereas the ray representation has a larger depth discrepancy but a floor of only \SIrange{50}{56}{\micro\strain}. The short-range endpoint-increment scales remain comparable, approximately \SIrange{0.20}{0.27}{\micro\metre}, showing that the difference is spatial structure rather than simply smaller point noise. Across gauge lengths and directions, exact virtual-gauge strain is reproduced by the longitudinal increment of the rigid-alignment residual with correlations close to unity. The compact CMO model, evaluated only on the wide acquisition from which it was identified, lies in the same mechanical-consistency band as the retrospective estimators rather than outperforming them. Physical interpretability and reprojection accuracy are therefore not substitutes for validation in the intended mechanical observable. Rigid-target sequences provide a practical zero-strain consistency test without requiring the rigid translation amplitude itself to be independently calibrated.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} stereo digital image correlation; stereo microscopy; calibration; apparent strain; ray field; non-central camera; virtual gauge; rigid-body motion.

\section{Introduction}
Stereo digital image correlation (stereo-DIC) reconstructs three-dimensional motion from two image sequences and then obtains strain from spatial variations of that motion. Calibration is upstream of both steps. It is therefore natural to rank calibrations by quantities that are directly available during fitting, such as pixel reprojection error, a target-coordinate residual, or pointwise depth error. These are necessary diagnostics, but they are not the final mechanical observable. Strain depends on differences between reconstruction errors at separated material points. A large common-mode position error can cancel almost completely in a gauge length, while a smaller but spatially coherent error gradient can generate a non-negligible false strain.

Calibration uncertainty has long been recognised as a contributor to the uncertainty of three-dimensional DIC \cite{reu2013}. The present work takes a complementary experimental route. Instead of prescribing a probability distribution on calibration parameters, we use rigid target motions for which the exact true strain is zero. The rigid displacement amplitude itself need not be known to establish that fact. The complete reconstruction chain can therefore be interrogated directly in the quantity of interest: change of distance between reconstructed material points.

Microscope stereo vision makes the distinction particularly visible. Schreier et al. \cite{schreier2004} established stereo vision under light microscopy, while polynomial mappings derived from the Soloff formulation \cite{soloff1997} remain an effective black-box calibration strategy and are implemented in PYCASO \cite{pycaso2023}. At the same time, a general optical system need not be central. Vision-ray calibration \cite{bothe2010} and generic camera models \cite{schops2020} instead associate each image coordinate with a three-dimensional viewing ray. A CMO stereomicroscope is a concrete case in which forcing two single optical centres can be structurally inappropriate because both image channels share a main objective and use off-axis sub-apertures.

This configuration exposes two distinct questions. The optical question is how to identify a compact, interpretable CMO model when direct joint optimisation of optical parameters and target poses is poorly conditioned. The mechanical question is which calibration best preserves the distances from which strain will be derived. The first question motivates a flexible ray field as an intermediate geometric observable. The second requires validation as a function of gauge length and direction.

The contribution of this paper is therefore not a claim that a ray model is universally more accurate. The contribution is a validation framework that separates pointwise and differential calibration performance. A measured ray field is first used to diagnose the optical family and identify a compact physical CMO model. Rigid translations are then converted into a scale- and direction-dependent apparent-strain test. Finally, the measured strain is linked quantitatively to the spatial increment of the non-rigid reconstruction residual. The central proposition is that \emph{pointwise calibration accuracy is neither necessary nor sufficient for strain accuracy; the mechanically relevant quantity is the spatial increment of reconstruction error over the measurement gauge.}

\section{Ray-space identification of the CMO calibration}
\label{sec:cmo}
\subsection{Non-central representation}
The archived instrument is a Leica M205C CMO stereomicroscope. For channel $c\in\{L,R\}$, each image coordinate $(u,v)$ is represented by a three-dimensional line
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_c(u,v)=\{O_c(u,v)+\lambda d_c(u,v):\lambda\in\mathbb R\},
\label{eq:rayline}
\end{equation}
where $O_c$ is one point on the line and $d_c$ its unit direction. The point $O_c$ is gauge-dependent along the ray, but the line itself is the observable. This representation does not require every ray to pass through a unique camera centre.

On the tested configuration, the conventional central stereo calibration used as a baseline remains above \SI{300}{px} reprojection error. This result is used only as evidence that the tested central family is structurally inadequate for this instrument; it is not a general statement about conventional stereo calibration.

\subsection{Measure rays first, identify optics second}
A direct physical bundle adjustment couples optics and calibration-target poses. Near-parallel planar views allow pose changes to compensate several optical directions. To reduce this ambiguity, the calibration is split into two stages. First, a flexible Zernike ray field is fitted to ChArUco observations. Second, compact physical hypotheses are compared with that measured field using direction and Pl\"ucker-moment residuals.

The diagnostic progression is important. A perspective CMO skeleton leaves a structured angular residual. A quasi-telecentric skeleton removes most of that structure but leaves a low-order, nearly spatially uniform component. Allowing one rigid transform per optical arm removes this global component and yields the 26-parameter CMO model: 14 quasi-telecentric parameters plus two six-parameter rigid arm corrections. These extra degrees of freedom are therefore motivated by the topology of the residual, not solely by a reduction of a scalar cost.

On the ten physical-calibration views the 26-parameter model reaches \SI{1.06}{px} RMS (median \SI{0.87}{px}, 95th percentile \SI{1.84}{px}), while the 57-parameter flexible ray field reaches approximately \SI{0.47}{px}. The lower flexible residual is expected and is not interpreted as metric ground truth. The physical model is retained because it gives a compact description of the measured non-central geometry and a reproducible ray generator for subsequent triangulation.

\subsection{Observability}
The near-parallel calibration poses constrain lateral geometry much more strongly than some axial directions. A Schur-complement analysis of the joint optical-pose information matrix identifies weak optical modes after pose marginalisation. A direct bundle adjustment can therefore improve pixel RMS while moving along compensation directions that change physical descriptors. The ray-field stage partly avoids this failure mode by separating geometric measurement from physical interpretation, but it does not eliminate metric gauge limitations. This distinction becomes important again in the independent surface comparison of Section~\ref{sec:surface}.

\section{Mechanical validation framework}
\label{sec:framework}
\subsection{Rigid motion as a zero-strain experiment}
Let $\widehat{\mathbf X}_0(\mathbf x)$ denote the reconstructed reference position of target location $\mathbf x$, and $\widehat{\mathbf X}_t(\mathbf x)$ its reconstruction after motion. The true rigid object satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathbf X_t(\mathbf x)=\mathbf R_t\mathbf X_0(\mathbf x)+\mathbf T_t,
\qquad \mathbf R_t\in SO(3).
\end{equation}
For each reconstructed frame we fit the best proper rigid transform between reconstructed point clouds and define the remaining non-rigid residual
\begin{equation}
\mathbf e_t(\mathbf x)=
\widehat{\mathbf X}_t(\mathbf x)-
[\mathbf R_t\widehat{\mathbf X}_0(\mathbf x)+\mathbf T_t],
\label{eq:rigidresidual}
\end{equation}
No scale and no reflection is allowed in this alignment. Therefore any deformation retained by Eq.~\eqref{eq:rigidresidual} is reconstruction-induced and should vanish for a perfect calibration.
